# How I want my cover letters written

The AI reads this guide each time it writes a cover letter. Change it to suit you.

## What the letter is for

My resume shows what I've done. The letter does what the resume can't:

- It opens with the ad's most telling line, and why it fits me.
- It answers what the ad values, with one true story for each need.
- It answers what the ad asks, and deals honestly with a missing must-have.

## Facts come first

- Use only facts from me: my master profile, my notes, my answers and the approved brief, when given. Unsure? Leave it out.
- My master profile decides what's true and how to describe me. Follow its rules on job titles, years, numbers and their labels, wording to avoid, the "Don't claim" items always, and the "To confirm" and ⚠ items unless my answers settle them.
- This guide decides how to write the letter. Where my profile's notes on length, structure or style differ, follow this guide. The ad's instructions beat this guide.
- About the employer, say only what the ad says. Don't add products, news, awards or values from memory.
- Skip any steps in my profile that don't fit here, such as asking me questions, making a Word file or updating the profile.

## Step 1: plan it (in your thinking, before you write)

When an approved brief is given, it's the plan: follow it in place of this step, but still note items 1 and 3. With no brief, plan it yourself, one short line per item.

1. The role title, the employer and any reference number, exactly as the ad writes them.
2. Who to write to (see Layout).
3. Anything the ad asks applicants to address in the letter: questions, selection criteria, "tell us about…". Note their order and any word or page limit.
4. The two or three things the ad values most, not only its skills list. For each one, my best story.
5. The ad's most telling line, word for word, and why it fits me.
6. Any must-have I don't have, with my nearest real evidence.

## Step 2: write it

### Length

- One page: about 250 to 400 words, not counting the header, date and sign-off.
- Three to five short paragraphs of two to four sentences each.
- If the ad asks me to answer questions in the letter, answer every one in full, even if that takes the letter to 400 words.
- If the ad sets a limit, keep to it.

### Layout

Use these blocks, in this order. The brackets are placeholders.

1. `# [My name]`
2. My contact line, the same as on my resume.
3. The date you're given, for example "29 October 2026".
4. `**Re: [Role title as the ad writes it], [Employer, or the recruiter's firm if the client isn't named]**`, with the reference number if the ad has one.
5. `Dear [Contact's name],`
6. The paragraphs.
7. `Kind regards,`
8. My name.

- If there's only an email, a first name in their email counts as their name: sam@example.com.au is "Dear Sam,". But jobs@ or info@ isn't a name.
- For a recruiter's ad ("We're representing…", "our client"), write to the recruiter, not the employer.
- With no name at all, write "Dear Hiring Manager,". Never write "To whom it may concern", "Dear Sir/Madam", or "Mr", "Ms" or "Mrs".
- Leave a blank line between every block. Without it, Markdown joins the lines into one paragraph.
- No headings inside the letter, no tables and no bullet points. Bold only the "Re:" line and the lead-ins to answers (see below).

### Paragraph by paragraph

1. **Opening, two or three sentences.** Start with the ad's most telling line, in its own words, then why it fits me, backed by a fact. Never restate the skills list, or the role (the "Re:" line names it). A made-up example (never copy its facts), for an ad that says "Our riders come first": "Your riders come first. Mine did too at Example Transit, where I cut its ticket app's checkout from five steps to two."
2. **Stories, one or two paragraphs.** Answer what the ad values, not only its skills: one short story per need (the brief's needs, or else the ad's two or three biggest). Each has the problem, what I did, and the result (a number or a named outcome). Name the client and the product as my profile allows. No strings of tool names. Don't copy a resume bullet. A made-up example (never copy its facts): "Example Co's booking app froze every Monday. I traced it to one slow search and rebuilt it. Monday complaints stopped within a week."
3. **What the ad asks the letter to answer, if anything.** Answer in the ad's order. Start each answer with a short bold lead-in that names the topic, for example "**Drupal and PHP.**" Then give a short story, as above.
4. **A missing must-have, only if there is one.** Put it near the end, just before the close. Name it once, plainly, without apology. Give my nearest real evidence, including any self-study my profile records. No stock plan: the evidence is the answer. With no real evidence, leave it out. Don't mention missing "nice to have" items.
5. **Close, one to three sentences.** If the opening hasn't linked what the ad says about the employer to my experience, add one true sentence that does (no flattery, no company history). Say I'd welcome a conversation. Add my availability, notice period or work rights only if my profile states them. If the ad lists checks (such as a police check or a Working with Children Check), you can say I'm happy to complete them.

The order can bend: a gap can sit inside the answer to a question about it.

### Government and council jobs

- If the ad wants a separate statement against the selection criteria, keep the letter a short pitch: why this role, why this organisation, and what I offer. Don't answer the criteria in the letter.
- Mention the organisation's values only with an example of me acting on them.

### Tone

- Write in the first person: plain, confident and warm, with contractions (I'm, I've, I'd). Write the way a capable person talks to a colleague they respect.
- Write more about their needs than about my goals. Don't start every sentence with "I".
- Mix short and longer sentences. Use "is" and "has", not "serves as" or "stands as".
- Use Australian spelling: organise, prioritise, colour, centre, analyse, licence (the noun), program. Keep product names as their makers spell them.

### What to avoid

These make a letter sound generic or AI-written:

- **Openings:** "I am writing to apply…", "I am excited (or thrilled) to…", "Please accept this letter…", "My name is…".
- **Words:** passionate, dynamic, self-starter, dependable, people person, team player, hard-working, results-driven, proven track record, unique blend, incredible, seamless, cutting-edge, synergy, delve, tapestry, testament, pivotal, crucial, showcase, underscore, vibrant, landscape, leverage, spearhead, foster, elevate, empower, journey, "aligns perfectly", "I believe", "I feel".
- **Habits:**
  - "Not just X, but Y".
  - An "-ing" phrase tacked onto the end of a sentence (", ensuring quality").
  - Lists of three out of habit.
  - Em dashes (—). Use a full stop or a comma instead.
- **Content:** salary, criticism of a past employer, my life story, a summary of my whole career.

## Step 3: check it (in your thinking, before you reply)

Fix anything that fails. Then reply with the letter only.

1. The employer's name, the role title and any reference number match the ad exactly.
2. Every question or criterion the ad asks the letter to address has an answer, in the ad's order, with an example.
3. Each need has one short story with its result. No strings of tool names.
4. Each missing must-have with real evidence is named once, near the end, with that evidence and no stock plan. No missing "nice to have" is mentioned.
5. Every fact (title, employer, date, number, tool) comes from me, with its label kept ("about", "estimate"). Nothing from "Don't claim". Nothing about the employer that the ad doesn't say.
6. No sentence copies a resume bullet.
7. It's 250 to 400 words and fits on one page, within any limit the ad sets.
8. The greeting follows Layout, and the opening uses the ad's most telling line. Contractions, Australian spelling and the date as given. None of the words or habits to avoid.
