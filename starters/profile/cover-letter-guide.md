# How I want my cover letters written

The AI reads this guide each time it writes a cover letter. Change it to suit you.

## What the letter is for

My resume shows what I've done. The letter does what the resume can't:

- It shows, in the first two sentences, why I match this job's biggest need.
- It gives the reasons and the method behind one or two results.
- It answers anything the ad asks applicants to address.
- It deals honestly with a missing requirement.

Most hiring staff say they read cover letters, and tailored letters get more calls than generic ones. But they now see many AI-written letters, so stock phrases count against me. True, specific detail is what stands out.

## Facts come first

- Use only facts from my master profile. If you're not sure of something, leave it out. You can't ask me questions here.
- My master profile decides what's true and how to describe me. Follow its rules on job titles, years, numbers and their labels, wording to avoid, and the "Don't claim", "To confirm" and ⚠ items.
- This guide decides how to write the letter. My profile may have its own notes on length, structure or style. Where they differ from this guide, follow this guide.
- About the employer, say only what the ad says. Don't add products, news, awards or values from memory: you may have them wrong.
- Skip any steps in my profile that don't fit here, such as asking me questions, making a Word file or updating the profile.

## Step 1: plan it (in your thinking, before you write)

Keep the plan short: one line per item.

1. The role title, the employer and any reference number, exactly as the ad writes them.
2. The person to address, if the ad names a contact.
3. Anything the ad asks applicants to address in the letter: questions, selection criteria, "tell us about…". Note their order and any word or page limit.
4. The ad's two or three biggest needs. For each one, the single best example in my profile.
5. Any must-have I don't have. For each one, my nearest real evidence and proof that I learn fast.
6. What the ad says about the employer: its work, its users or community, its values. Pick one true link to my experience.

## Step 2: write it

### Length

- One page: about 250 to 400 words, not counting the header, date and sign-off.
- Three to five short paragraphs of two to four sentences each.
- If the ad asks me to answer questions in the letter, answer every one in full, even if that takes the letter to 400 words.
- If the ad sets a limit, keep to it. The ad's instructions beat this guide.

### Layout

Use these blocks, in this order. The brackets are placeholders.

1. `# [My name]`
2. My contact line, the same as on my resume.
3. The date you're given, for example "29 October 2026".
4. `**Re: [Role title as the ad writes it], [Employer]**`, with the reference number if the ad has one.
5. `Dear [Contact's first and last name],`
6. The paragraphs.
7. `Kind regards,`
8. My name.

- If the ad names no contact, write "Dear Hiring Manager,".
- Never write "To whom it may concern", "Dear Sir/Madam", or "Mr", "Ms" or "Mrs". You can't know which title is right.
- Leave a blank line between every block. Without it, Markdown joins the lines into one paragraph.
- No headings inside the letter, no tables and no bullet points. Bold only the "Re:" line and the lead-ins to answers (see below).

### Paragraph by paragraph

1. **Opening, two or three sentences.** The "Re:" line already names the role, so don't repeat it. Open with my strongest match to the ad's biggest need, backed by a fact. For example, in made-up words (never copy its facts): "You need someone to move a busy booking site onto a new platform without breaking it for its users. I led that kind of move for a national retailer last year."
2. **Evidence, one or two paragraphs.** Give two or three examples, each tied to one of the ad's biggest needs. For each one, say what the problem was, what I did, and the result (a number or a named outcome from my profile). Then say why it matters for this job. Add what the resume can't show: the context, why I made a decision, how I worked with people. Don't copy a resume bullet.
3. **The ad's questions, if it has any.** Answer them in the ad's order. Start each answer with a short bold lead-in that names the topic, for example "**Drupal and PHP.**" Give a specific example in two to four sentences: the situation, what I did, and the result.
4. **A missing must-have, only if there is one.** Name it once, plainly, without apology. Give my nearest real evidence. Then give a concrete plan to close the gap, and an example of me learning a new tool fast. Never skip the plan: naming a gap with no answer does more harm than saying nothing. Don't mention missing "nice to have" items.
5. **Fit, one or two sentences.** One true, specific link between what the ad says about the employer and my experience. No flattery and no company history.
6. **Close, one or two sentences.** Say I'd welcome a conversation. Add my availability, notice period or work rights only if my profile states them. If the ad lists checks (such as a police check or a Working with Children Check), you can say I'm happy to complete them.

The order can bend. Fit can go in the opening, and a gap can sit inside the answer to a question about it.

### Government and council jobs

- Follow the ad's instructions exactly.
- If the ad wants a separate statement against the selection criteria, keep the letter a short pitch: why this role, why this organisation, and what I offer. Don't try to answer every criterion in the letter.
- If the ad asks me to address criteria in the letter, answer each one under a bold lead-in, with an example.
- Mention the organisation's values only with an example of me acting on them.

### Tone

- Write in the first person: plain, confident and warm. Write the way a capable person talks to a colleague they respect.
- Write more about their needs than about my goals. Don't start every sentence with "I".
- Mix short and longer sentences. Use "is" and "has", not "serves as" or "stands as".
- Use Australian spelling: organise, prioritise, colour, centre, analyse, licence (the noun), program. Keep product names as their makers spell them.

### What to avoid

These make a letter sound generic or AI-written:

- **Openings:** "I am writing to apply…", "I am excited (or thrilled) to…", "Please accept this letter…", "My name is…".
- **Words:** passionate, dynamic, self-starter, dependable, people person, team player, hard-working, results-driven, proven track record, unique blend, incredible, seamless, cutting-edge, synergy, delve, tapestry, testament, pivotal, crucial, showcase, underscore, vibrant, landscape, leverage, spearhead, foster, elevate, empower, journey, "aligns perfectly", "I believe", "I feel".
- **Habits:**
  - "Not just X, but Y".
  - An "-ing" phrase tacked onto the end of a sentence (", ensuring quality").
  - Lists of three out of habit.
  - Em dashes (—). Use a full stop or a comma instead.
- **Content:** salary, criticism of a past employer, my life story, a summary of my whole career.

## Step 3: check it (in your thinking, before you reply)

Fix anything that fails. Then reply with the letter only.

1. The employer's name, the role title and any reference number match the ad exactly.
2. Every question or criterion the ad asks the letter to address has an answer, in the ad's order, with an example.
3. Each of the ad's two or three biggest needs has a specific example.
4. Each missing must-have is named once, with a plan. No missing "nice to have" is mentioned.
5. Every fact (title, employer, date, number, tool) comes from my profile, with its label kept ("about", "estimate"). Nothing from "Don't claim". Nothing about the employer that the ad doesn't say.
6. No sentence copies a resume bullet.
7. It's 250 to 400 words and fits on one page, within any limit the ad sets.
8. The opening isn't a stock phrase. None of the words or habits to avoid. Australian spelling. The date as given.
